A platform for autonomous electron microscopy

The model plans.
The compiler decides.

TEM-Scientist is an autonomy stack for transmission electron microscopy: experimental goals compile into typed, statically verified protocols, rehearsed against a physics-aware digital twin before a single lens current changes. At execution time, no language model is in the loop.

§01BackgroundWhy autonomy · why not an agent loop

The bottleneck is not the microscope

An aberration-corrected TEM can resolve individual atomic columns and distinguish oxygen from hafnium. What it cannot do is decide what to look at next. That decision — which region, which modality, which tilt, when to stop — sits with a human expert standing at the console, and it is the scarcest resource in the building.

Constraint 01

Beam time is rationed

A user facility instrument is booked in shifts and oversubscribed. Alignment, tuning, and search consume much of a session before the first useful image is recorded. Experiments end when the clock runs out, not when the question is answered.

Sessions end on schedule, not on evidence
Constraint 02

Measurement is destructive

The probe that images the specimen also damages it. Knock-on displacement and radiolysis change the very structure under study, so a wasted acquisition is not merely slow — it can spend the sample. There is often no second attempt at the same region.

Dose is a budget you cannot refund
Constraint 03

The method is tacit

Why an operator escalated to ABF, retuned the corrector, or abandoned a particle rarely survives into the methods section. The reasoning stays in the operator's head and the lab notebook, which is why microscopy results are hard to reproduce across instruments and people.

Decisions vanish; only images remain

Why not simply put an agent at the console?

Language models are good at exactly the part that is scarce — proposing what to try next. But a stochastic planner in direct control of a multi-million-dollar instrument, aimed at an irreplaceable specimen, fails in ways that matter. The fix is not a better prompt; it is a change in where the model sits.

Agent in the control loop
  • Actions are sampled at run time, so the same goal can produce different instrument behavior on every attempt
  • A hallucinated command surfaces as a hardware action, with no stage between proposal and lens current
  • Failures are discovered on the specimen, after the dose has already been delivered
  • What ran is reconstructed from a transcript — not an artifact you can diff, hash, or replay
  • Refusal degrades into improvisation: an unanswerable goal still yields something
Model as compiler front-end
  • The model emits a protocol, not an action; execution replays that artifact bit-identically
  • Hallucinated primitives and unbound post-conditions are rejected statically, before rehearsal
  • Failures surface in a physics-aware twin, where the only cost is compute
  • Every run is a hashed, schema-validated object, archived beside the data it produced
  • An unsatisfiable goal fails to compile — a refusal with a paper trail, not a plausible substitute

The objective is not a microscope that runs itself. It is a microscope whose experiments are written down: compiled from a stated hypothesis, checked before they run, executed identically every time, and archived as artifacts that another lab can read, replay, and disagree with. Autonomy is the means; reproducible experimental method is the end.

Nothing in that machinery is specific to a material. The protocol schema, the lint suite, the twin, and the escalation policy describe how a TEM experiment is decided, not what it is about — so the same stack should serve interface chemistry, defect populations, in-situ dynamics, and beam-sensitive specimens. Ferroelectric HfO₂ is the test case we develop against because it is unforgiving: the structure of interest is nearly invisible to the modality an operator would reach for first.

§02ArchitectureCompile → Verify → Rehearse → Execute → Reflect

A compiler pipeline, not a chat loop

Every experiment passes through five stages. The language model contributes at the ends — compiling goals and reflecting on outcomes. The middle is deterministic machinery.

Fig. 1 — The compile–rehearse–execute–reflect loop. Model involvement (blue-bordered boxes) is confined to compilation and reflection. Everything between the lint gate and the instrument is deterministic: amber paths are rejections that either recompile with bounded diagnosis or terminate as an auditable refusal; the green path is the only route to hardware.

Stage 1

Compile

A natural-language goal is compiled into a typed protocol: a finite state machine over a closed vocabulary of safe microscope primitives, each step carrying explicit pre-conditions and ledger-keyed post-conditions, plus an objective contract binding the protocol to the goal.

Stage 2

Verify

Three layers: schema conformance against protocol.schema.json, static lints over the FSM, and an immutable objective contract with fidelity checks C1–C6 / D1–D3. Hallucinated primitives, non-ledger post-condition keys, and non-terminating states are rejected before rehearsal.

L1–L15 landed · L16–L17 queued
Stage 3

Rehearse

The full protocol runs against TWIN, a physics-aware behavioral simulator of the target instrument. Sensitivity and specificity of the gate are characterized by mutation testing: 11 fault classes plus 2 control classes injected into admitted protocols.

TWIN · 11+2 mutation classes
Stage 4

Execute

The verified protocol runs deterministically with zero model involvement. Safety invariants are enforced below the MCP layer, in the runtime itself — an unsafe action is unrepresentable, not merely discouraged.

Stage 5

Reflect

Execution traces are persisted and fed back for headroom analysis: which obligations were discharged, which post-conditions failed, and whether the next protocol should escalate modality or refuse.

§03Static verificationLint registry · schema-coupled · CI-enforced

The lint gate

Every compiled protocol must clear the full lint suite before it may touch TWIN, let alone hardware. Manuscript examples are held to the same standard: CI extracts every lstlisting[style=proto] block from the paper's sources and validates it against the published schema.

$$\mathrm{Admit}(\pi) \;\iff\; \underbrace{\bigwedge_{i=1}^{15} L_i(\pi)}_{\textsf{static}} \;\;\wedge\;\; \underbrace{\mathrm{TWIN}(\pi) \models \mathcal{C}(G)}_{\textsf{rehearsed}}$$

For a compiled protocol π: a conjunction, not a score. One failing lint or one unmet contract clause blocks the protocol outright — there is no partial credit, no confidence threshold, and no override path from a rejected protocol to the instrument.

LintClassRejects
L1–L4StructuralSchema violations: missing required fields (including objective_contract_hash), malformed state ids, dangling transitions, unreachable states.
L5–L8SemanticType errors on primitive arguments, out-of-range setpoints, pre-conditions that no reachable predecessor can establish.
L9–L11Safety / terminationMissing terminal obligations — e.g. a tilt series that never returns the stage to zero — plus a restricted expression grammar (L10) and parameter binding (L11) that close off free-form computation in protocol bodies.
L12–L15Contract groundingGrounds the objective contract itself: rejects references to non-existent primitives and post-condition keys absent from the ledger. Applied statically to frozen contracts — no execution required — it flags every task that failed, plus three that passed, revealing the defect to be more widespread than the score suggested.
L16–L17QueuedSetpoints & obligationsValidates setpoints against the instrument profile (L16) and counts obligations discharged against those declared (L17) — the latter aimed squarely at silent substitution, where a requirement is dropped rather than reported unsatisfiable.
L19Value rangesCloses the blind spot the fault study exposed: the gate checked structure and identifiers but never magnitudes, so a well-formed, correctly-named, physically wrong setpoint passed. Held-out fault detection with L19 in place rises from 33/38 to 38/38 — the escapes were all value errors.
§04GuaranteesWhat holds by construction

Autonomy you can audit

Determinism

Deterministic action selection

Diversity lives at compile time and stops there: 27 of 31 goals compile to structurally distinct protocols (mean distance 0.315), yet all 30 admitted protocols select actions reproducibly. We state this as deterministic action selection rather than "execution determinism" — fixed-seed traces are identical partly by construction, and the honest test is behavior under seed sweeps, observation replay, and perturbation near contingency thresholds.

Verification

Schema-coupled by CI

Every protocol validates against protocol.schema.json, including a required objective-contract hash. Even the manuscript's examples are extracted and validated in CI, so the paper can't drift from the system.

Evaluation

Locked, pre-registered, honest

Repairs are predicted before they are applied and reported whichever way they move. When the contract repair raised the locked score but broke a refusal, we published the lower figure — the pipeline's own unmodified output would have read higher and hidden it. Four defects in the scoring apparatus are documented, alongside an ablation showing that removing the contract layer raises the measured score — reported rather than quietly banked.

§05ProtocolsTyped FSMs · deterministic runtime · refusable

Protocols are the unit of trust

A protocol is not a transcript of model output — it is a compiled program: a finite state machine over a closed primitive vocabulary, with pre- and post-conditions stated over the instrument ledger.

$$\pi \;=\; \bigl(\,S,\; s_0,\; \delta,\; \mathrm{Pre},\; \mathrm{Post},\; h\,\bigr) \qquad \delta \,:\, S \times \Sigma \longrightarrow S$$
Σ
the closed vocabulary of instrument primitives — nothing outside it is expressible
Pre, Post
predicates over instrument ledger keys, checked before and after every state
h
objective_contract_hash — binds this protocol irreversibly to the goal it was compiled from

Diversity is a compile-time property; reproducibility is a runtime one. Across 31 goals, 27 compile to structurally distinct FSMs (mean pairwise sequence distance 0.315) that all pass their checkers — while all 30 admitted protocols select actions reproducibly on replay. Many valid programs, one behavior each.

Goals that cannot be satisfied safely don't degrade into improvisation. They fail to compile, or the objective contract — bound to the protocol by a required objective_contract_hash — is rejected by the verifier: a refusal with a paper trail.

Because protocols are data, they can be diffed, hashed, replayed against the twin, and archived beside the micrographs they produced.

§06Locked evaluation10 held-out tasks × 5 repeats · checker-first · prompt-freeze-v1

Measured, not vibes

The held-out unit is the task, not the attempt: 10 locked tasks with checkers authored before the system was frozen, run 5× each. Statistics are reported clustered — treating repeats as independent overstates precision by nearly 2× (cluster-bootstrap 0.34–0.88 against Clopper–Pearson 0.47–0.75). Bugs found in frozen artifacts are recorded rather than patched mid-run, and where a correction had to be made by hand, both the corrected and uncorrected runs are published with per-row reasons.

The aggregate is the least interesting number here. Pre-repair performance was bimodal (ICC 0.77): five tasks at 5/5, three at 0/5, two in between — and all 15 unsatisfiable-contract failures fell inside exactly those three never-solved tasks. That turned the repair into a falsifiable prediction about which tasks should move, registered before it was applied.

The prediction held — and cost us something. The root cause was a one-line asymmetry: the protocol stage received tool contracts, the contract stage did not. Supplying them raised the locked suite from 31/50 to 37/50 and recovered most of the predicted failures. But the same repair converted one correct refusal into a silent substitution: rather than reporting a requirement it could no longer express as unsatisfiable, the compiler quietly dropped it. Those five compilations score as failures — and no longer by hand: outcome classification is now automated over typed compile outcomes, so the 37/50 is reproducible from the artifacts rather than adjudicated by the authors.

Run end to end on the original harness, the pipeline would have reported 42/50 and concealed the regression. We publish 37/50 and both runs. The correction began as a manual edit — an uncomfortable position for a paper arguing that measurement layers need scrutiny — which is precisely why it was replaced by typed outcome classification that any reader can re-run.

§07MeasurementWho checks the checkers

The measurement layer failed four times

A system that verifies protocols is only as trustworthy as the apparatus that scores it — and in this work that apparatus was wrong four separate times. Each defect stayed invisible until an unrelated change exposed it, and each sat in a layer whose own correctness nothing checked. We report them because the base rate suggests there are others.

Harness ruledefect 1 An identifier-prefix rule in the scoring runner credited every locked compile failure as a correct refusal. Caught and disclosed; the corrected figure is the one reported.
Vacuous checkerdefect 2 The refusal checker passed a protocol that had abandoned the requested constraint. The structural problem: a correct refusal produces no execution trace, so a trace-based checker cannot distinguish it from any other absence of execution. The assertion required — that nothing was admitted, that the compiler declared the request unsatisfiable, that no dose was spent — is about the compile outcome, and belongs in the harness rather than the checker library. That restructuring has not been done, so this refusal result rests on inspection of compile artifacts rather than an automated check.
Analyser blind spotdefect 3 The contract-grounding analyser written to catch unsatisfiable requirements reported one such contract as grounded — because it read only one of the two spellings the contract stage emits.
Outage scored as refusaldefect 4 An API outage mid-run caused infrastructure failures to be recorded as correct refusals. A checker that infers refusal from the absence of a result cannot tell a principled refusal from a network error — the same structural flaw as defect 2, surfacing through a different door.

Fig. 7 — Two layers, two kinds of justification. An ablation that only ever confirms the design teaches nothing. These disagree: rehearsal is vindicated by the score, the contract layer is convicted by it. A layer whose removal improves your headline number is either dead weight or evidence that the metric is too permissive — and here the rejected protocols are ones the checkers should not have accepted.

The results that argue against the system are the ones worth reading. A held-out fault study caught 33 of 38 injected faults and localized every escape to the same blind spot: the gate validated structure and identifiers but never values, so a well-formed, correctly-named, physically wrong setpoint passed. Adding the L19 value-range lint takes detection to 38/38 — the fix is real, and so is the fact that the gate shipped without it for as long as it did.

Three distinct mechanisms sit beneath the contract failures — ungrounded names, scoping limitations, and arithmetic errors — which is why a single repair moved the score without emptying the category. And the ablation corrected an error in our own published diagnosis: LK09 fails at grounding, not at post-condition checking as the paper had claimed. A diagnosis overturned by the experiment meant to confirm it is the most useful thing an ablation can produce.

Held-out fault study · contract & rehearsal ablations

Artifact provenance is the quiet systemic issue: scoreboard paths overwritten, rejected drafts co-located with admitted protocols, rehearsal reports written under the wrong filename — seven distinct instances in one revision cycle. Every headline number on this page depends on knowing which file produced it.

We draw no comfort from having found all four. The systematic remedy — a held-out evaluation of the evaluation apparatus itself — is not something we have done, and we say so rather than implying the measurement layer is now sound.

§08Instrument stackSpectra 300 · layered control · invariants below MCP

From protocol to lens current

Hardware execution uses layered control bridged over the lab LAN. Every layer above the invariant boundary can fail arbitrarily without the instrument leaving its safe envelope.

Fig. 6 — Why the reflex layer cannot be a model call. Five orders of magnitude separate contingency response from compilation. The earlier reported figure of 0.012 ms was a display artifact of the timing harness; the measured median is 6.6 µs, with a worst case of 10.4 µs over 200 trials. Anything that must react to a drifting stage or a saturating detector has to live below the compiler, which is why safety sits in the runtime rather than in the plan.

Fig. 2 — Layered instrument control. The reflex layer answers in \(6.6\,\mu\mathrm{s}\) against a \(2114\,\mathrm{ms}\) median compile latency — a ratio of \(\sim\!3.2\times10^{5}\), which is why contingency response cannot wait on a model. Verified protocols descend through the stack; safety invariants sit between the runtime and every vendor API, so nothing above the boundary — including the runtime itself — can command an action outside the safe envelope.

§09Science loopHypothesis → discriminability → modality escalation

The instrument chooses its detector for a reason

Every TEM question is really a question about which signal carries the answer. Light-element order, strain, valence, magnetic and polar structure, short-range disorder — each is legible in some modalities and invisible in others, and the ladder from imaging to diffraction to 4D acquisition costs more dose and time at every rung. TEM-Scientist compiles that decision: a hypothesis is scored against forward simulations, and the protocol stops at the first modality that can actually discriminate it.

$$m^{\star} \;=\; \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{m \,\in\, \mathcal{M}} \; \mathrm{cost}(m) \qquad \text{s.t.} \qquad D_m(H) \;\ge\; \tau$$
𝓜
the modality ladder — HAADF, ABF/iDPC, 4D-STEM, CBED
Dm(H)
discriminability of hypothesis H under modality m, scored against forward simulation rather than guessed at the console
cost(m)
monotone in dose, acquisition time, and data volume — which is what makes stopping early worth something
τ
the discrimination threshold; only D and τ change between materials, never the machinery around them

Fig. 3 — Modality escalation, grounded in physics. The ladder is material-agnostic: what changes between problems is the order parameter that defines discrimination, not the machinery around it. Worked test case: a 180° ferroelectric wall in HfO₂ registers only ~8% contrast in HAADF but is resolvable through the oxygen sublattice in ABF/iDPC — the wall located at ~20.08 Å by a vector order parameter, against a 20.1 Å manual ground truth. Caveat earned the hard way: in the first simulation run ABF contrast was zero — not from dose or detector geometry but because the polarization axis lay parallel to the beam, making the signal invisible by construction. Specimen orientation is therefore part of the ground truth, and any escalation policy that ignores zone axis will escalate for the wrong reason.

The escalation policy is one mechanism serving many questions. Ferroelectric HfO₂ exercises the hardest case — a structure invisible to the default modality — which is why it is the test bed rather than the subject.
